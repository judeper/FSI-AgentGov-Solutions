A business user can stand up a Copilot Studio or Agent Builder agent in an afternoon — with no security review, no accountable owner, and no record of the decision. In one sentence

agent-intake is a pre-build gate: before a business user (a maker) can create an AI agent, they submit a short request; the system classifies its risk, routes the right approval, and writes a permanent, non-editable record of the decision — then hands the approved agent to identity, registry, and monitoring systems automatically.

The shift

The build button moved to the business — the controls didn't follow

Building an AI agent has stopped being an IT project. With Copilot Studio and Microsoft Agent Builder, a business user can assemble an agent — wired to documents, data, and automated actions — in an afternoon. The capability is genuinely valuable. The gap is that the approval, ownership, and record-keeping that govern every other regulated system did not move with it.

What changed

A maker is any business user who builds an agent. They can now connect an agent to SharePoint sites, customer data, and actions taken on the firm's behalf without a security review, a named owner, or a written decision. The same request that would convene a change-control board for a traditional application can now happen silently in a chat window.

What goes wrong without a gate

When a user wants a new agent, somebody has to decide: is it low-risk enough for fast approval, or does it need security, compliance, and model-risk review? What data, environment, and retention class does it get? Is there a sponsor accountable for it? Without a structured intake, those decisions happen in chat threads, in tickets that never close, or not at all. These are the specific exposures that creates:

An agent built in a chat window has no named sponsor accountable for what it does. When something goes wrong — or an examiner asks who signed off — there is no answer. Supervision expectations assume a person, not a tool, owns the activity.

If the approval happened in a direct message or a ticket that was later closed, there is no durable, retrievable record of who decided what, when, and why. Books-and-records expectations assume that decision is captured and retained.

An agent can be pointed at non-public personal information (NPI) or material non-public information (MNPI) without anyone in privacy or compliance reviewing the data sources. Safeguards obligations assume sensitive data is identified and protected by design.

An agent can move data across country boundaries that the firm's data-residency policy does not permit — a quiet path to a privacy or contractual breach that no one chose deliberately.

An agent that recommends, prices, or approves is acting like a model. If it is never identified or tiered, it sits entirely outside the firm's model-risk-management program — exactly the blind spot supervisory model-risk guidance is written to close.

The exposures above multiply. The firm ends up with no single inventory of which agents exist, who owns them, or what they were approved to do — until the question is asked under examination.

The quiet failure mode

The danger is rarely one reckless agent. It is fifty reasonable-looking agents, each built in good faith, none of them inventoried, owned, or reviewed — discovered for the first time during an examination.

Regulatory stakes

What examiners expect — and where an ungoverned agent fails the test

Regulators do not need rules that name "AI agents." An agent that recommends, decides, touches customer data, or acts on the firm's behalf is already inside long-standing supervision, books-and-records, model-risk, and safeguards obligations. Here is what examiners expect for each — by name and section — and how the intake gate maps to it.

FINRA Rule 3110 — Supervision

What examiners expect: a named, accountable person supervises the activity and approved it. An agent built in a chat window has no such person. How the gate maps: every request routes to a designated sponsor who must attest before the agent is built — a one-click attestation on the Express path — and a maker cannot approve their own request (a default-deny). This supports compliance with FINRA Rule 3110. Implementation requires firms to confirm sponsor assignments match their supervisory structure.

FINRA Rule 4511 & SEC Rule 17a-4 — Books and records

What examiners expect: accurate records of business decisions, retained for a defined period in a non-rewritable form. How the gate maps: every decision is written to an immutable decision log (fsi_intakedecisionlog) under a 7-year Purview retention label (FSI-AgentIntake-7yr), capturing who asked, what the agent does, the risk classification, and the sponsor's timestamped attestation. This supports books-and-records expectations under FINRA Rule 4511 and helps meet the retention expectations of SEC Rule 17a-4. Implementation requires a Purview Compliance Admin to create the retention label.

OCC Bulletin 2011-12 & Federal Reserve SR 11-7 — Model risk

What examiners expect: models — including AI that recommends or decides — are identified, inventoried, and validated in proportion to their risk. How the gate maps: the risk triggers route model-bearing and high-impact requests onto the Full path, which includes a handoff to model risk management (the model-risk-management-automation solution). This aids in model-risk tiering consistent with OCC Bulletin 2011-12 and Federal Reserve SR 11-7. Implementation requires firms to align tiering thresholds with their MRM policy.

GLBA Section 501(b) — Safeguarding customer information

What examiners expect: non-public personal information is safeguarded by design. How the gate maps: three risk questions ask whether an agent will handle NPI, handle material non-public information, or move data across borders; any "Yes" — or even "Not sure" — routes the request out of the low-risk path to privacy and compliance review and zone / data-residency handling. This helps meet the safeguards expectations of GLBA Section 501(b). Implementation requires firms to confirm data-residency policy before production rollout.

Also referenced

The same retained decision log aids in the 7-year records-retention expectations of CFTC Rule 1.31, and the intake captures the firm-policy governance posture relevant to current OCC guidance on AI use. No single control satisfies any of these rules in isolation; the gate contributes the intake-stage evidence.

How it closes the loop

One gate, sized to the risk

The instinct is to add a review board. But a board in front of every request would bury low-risk work and push makers to route around the control. The design principle is risk-proportional: the effort to get approved scales with the risk of the agent. Every request follows the same five-stage path, then branches into one of three lanes.

Three lanes, one record

Six risk questions (does the agent initiate a financial transaction, face customers, run unmonitored, handle NPI, handle MNPI, or move data across borders) plus the intended audience determine the lane. The work effort is proportional to the answers.

For low-risk personal-agent requests where all six risk questions are "No" and the audience is personal-scope. The maker answers a 10-question form; the system auto-classifies; the sponsor receives a Teams card with FINRA Rule 3110 attestation language and approves in one click; the Microsoft Entra Agent ID and registry entry are provisioned automatically; InfoSec receives a passive 10% sample-audit notice. Elapsed time: minutes.

For mid-risk, team-scope requests (or one to two non-MNPI risk hits). The maker answers a 22-question catalog; the request routes to a conditional reviewer — InfoSec and Privacy, plus Compliance when the agent handles NPI — who can approve, request changes, or escalate.

For the highest-risk requests — firm-wide (enterprise) audiences, any material non-public information, three or more risk hits, or a Privacy-approved cross-border exception. A customer-facing agent that is not otherwise high-risk stays in Standard. The maker answers a 35-question catalog; the request enters a parallel reviewer board with quorum tracking (InfoSec, Privacy, Compliance, Legal, and Model Risk Management) and, where applicable, a model-risk handoff.

Conservative by default

A maker who answers "Not sure" is treated the same as "Yes." Uncertainty routes a request up to more review, never down — so the fast lane stays reserved for requests that are genuinely low-risk.

Two automatic stops

A request is denied by default when the sponsor and the maker are the same person (no self-approval), and when data would cross a border that the declared data-residency does not allow without a recorded privacy override. These are designed as safeguards; firms confirm the routing policy before production.

The lifecycle, end to end

  1. 1

    Submit

    The maker answers a risk-proportional catalog (10, 22, or 35 questions) in the intake portal — the firm's first point of maker onboarding.

  2. 2

    Classify

    The engine scores the six risk questions and the audience and assigns Express, Standard, or Full — or a default-deny when a safeguard fires.

  3. 3

    Approve

    The sponsor attests (Express) or the reviewer board reaches quorum (Standard and Full). A maker can never approve their own request.

  4. 4

    Record

    An immutable decision-log entry is written under the 7-year retention label — the examiner-ready evidence for that agent.

  5. 5

    Hand off

    On approval, the system mints the Microsoft Entra Agent ID, writes the registry entry, and notifies drift monitors — with no manual steps.

The value

Velocity with control — not a blocker

Governance that only adds friction gets bypassed, and a bypassed control is no control at all. This gate is built to deliver evidence and speed at the same time.

Every request produces one decision pack: the business case, the risk classification, the approver, the timestamp, and the attestation — written once, retained for seven years, and not editable after the fact. When an examiner asks "who approved this agent and why," the answer is a record, not a reconstruction.

Low-risk requests clear in minutes with a single sponsor click. Reviewer time is spent only on the requests that warrant it. Because the fast lane is genuinely fast, it removes the incentive that drives makers to route around the gate — which is what helps keep shadow agents from appearing in the first place.

On approval, every agent gets an accountable sponsor, a registry entry, and a Microsoft Entra Agent ID. The firm gains a single inventory of which agents exist, who owns them, and what they were approved to do — the opposite of the shadow-agent problem.

Approval automatically mints the Entra Agent ID, writes the registry record, and notifies drift monitors that watch for an agent later exceeding what it was approved to do. There are no manual handoffs to forget — the evidence and the controls travel together.

The intake gate is the primary implementation of framework controls 1.2 (Agent Registry), 1.7 (Comprehensive Audit Logging), and 2.13 (Documentation and Record-Keeping), and it contributes to 2.1 (Managed Environments) and 3.1 (Maker Onboarding and Training).

Recap & the ask

Key takeaways

Five things to carry out of this briefing.

What to remember

  • The ability to build AI agents has moved to business users; governance has to meet it before the build, not after.
  • An ungoverned agent already sits inside existing FINRA, SEC, OCC / Federal Reserve, and GLBA obligations — examiners will treat it that way.
  • A risk-proportional gate (Express / Standard / Full) puts the right amount of review on each request without blocking low-risk work.
  • Every decision becomes one immutable, examiner-ready record, retained for seven years, with an accountable sponsor and an automatic identity, registry, and monitoring handoff.
  • This is velocity with control — a fast lane for low risk and real scrutiny for high risk — not a blanket slowdown.

What we're asking you to validate

This solution is at v1.0.0-preview — suitable for pilot validation, not broad rollout, until the firm's own governance signs off. Five gates stand between the pilot and production:

  1. 1

    Walk the locked design decisions

    Review the product-owner-locked decisions — risk thresholds, the sponsor-sufficiency rule, and the deny gates — for fit with firm policy.

  2. 2

    Convene the cross-functional review

    The AI Governance Committee with InfoSec, Compliance, Legal, and IT architecture confirms the approach is right for the firm.

  3. 3

    Approve the access consent

    Grant admin consent for the documented Microsoft Graph permissions the workflow needs.

  4. 4

    Create the retention label

    A Purview Compliance Admin provisions the FSI-AgentIntake-7yr label so decision records are retained.

    Confirm that no undetected high-risk request slipped through the Express fast lane before scaling beyond pilot.
